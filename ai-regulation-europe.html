AI Regulation in Europe

What the European rules say about how AI agents must behave, which of them already apply, and what exists today to test it.

Status: research note, 5 November 2026. Dates and the state of each rule are as of November 2026.

Summary 1The rules today 2What agents may do 3By industry 3.1HR in depth 4LARA and the market Appendix

Summary

Situation

  • Europe has many rules for AI: the AI Act (in steps until Aug 2028), the GDPR, finance and security laws, and national rules in Germany and the Netherlands (section 1).
  • Some of them say how an AI agent must behave: do not manipulate people, say it is AI, respect privacy, keep a human in charge, and be tested (section 2).
  • Some of these already apply. Most rules for high-risk uses start in Dec 2027.

Problem

  • Companies are responsible when their agents break these rules, and for high-risk uses and personal data they must be able to show they comply. There is no official way to test agent behaviour yet: the only finished EU standard covers quality management.
  • Most tools test whether an agent works or is secure. Many map their results to the AI Act, but only a few test how an agent behaves against specific legal articles.

Where the need is biggest

  • Customer service and HR, because the rules apply now or from Dec 2027 (section 3).
  • In HR, bias testing exists (Warden AI, QuantPi, FairNow). For privacy, staff monitoring and human oversight, the only HR case found is custom tests Vijil built for one vendor (SmartRecruiters).
  • Banking and insurance come next.

LARA and the market

LARA, by Aithos (lara.aithos.org), checks whether an AI agent will break GDPR or the AI Act to finish a task.

  • LARA is not sold. A few companies test agents against specific articles (e.g. Promptfoo, LatticeFlow, Principled Intelligence) or check legal rules while the agent runs (e.g. Giskard, Alinia, Kyvvu). None publishes revenue for this. None was found whose scenarios are built so that the easiest way to finish the task breaks one specific article, as LARA's are; the closest is Principled Intelligence, still in private beta (section 4).
  • The money today is in evaluations sold to AI labs and large companies (Arena, Vals AI) and in agent security and certification (Gray Swan, AIUC).

1The rules on AI in Europe today

Three tables: the binding EU laws that set rules on how AI behaves, the other binding EU laws, then standards, guidance and Dutch and German rules.

1aEU laws that set rules on how AI behaves binding

LawKey points
AI ActAI behaviour · Product safety
  • Sets safety rules for AI. The more harm a use can do, the stricter the rules.
  • Since Feb 2025: AI may not manipulate people, exploit vulnerable people, score people on their behaviour, or read employees' emotions.
  • Since Aug 2026: people must be told when they are dealing with an AI.
  • From Dec 2027: AI used for high-risk tasks, such as hiring or credit scoring, must be tested, keep logs, have human oversight and be checked before it is sold.
  • From Aug 2028: for AI built into products, such as machines or medical devices.
  • Who it applies to: both companies that build AI and companies that use it, with builders carrying most of the duties. Makers of the largest models must already test them. Fines up to €35M or 7% of turnover.
GDPRAI behaviour · Privacy
  • Protects personal data (since 2018).
  • Companies need a valid reason to use personal data, may use only the data they need for that purpose, and must tell people what they do with it. If a use is risky, they must assess the risks first.
  • No important decision may be made only by AI without a way to get human review. Applies to any organisation processing personal data.
Consumer Credit Directive II
  • Consumer loans (from 20 Nov 2026).
  • Where credit decisions are automated, consumers can ask for human review and an explanation, and contest the decision.
  • Applies to lenders.
Platform Work Directive
  • Work arranged through digital platforms (e.g. delivery riders, taxi drivers, cleaners booked through an app).
  • Requires information on automated management of workers, human oversight, and human review of significant automated decisions.
  • Applies to digital labour platforms. National laws due 2 Dec 2026; neither Germany nor the Netherlands has passed one yet (Nov 2026).

1bOther binding EU laws IT security, liability, product safety

LawKey points
DORA
  • IT risk in financial services (since Jan 2025).
  • Requires IT risk management, incident reporting and resilience testing.
  • Applies to banks, insurers and other financial firms. Their IT suppliers must accept audit and testing rights in contracts.
NIS2
  • Cybersecurity in 18 critical sectors (insurance is not one).
  • Requires security measures including supplier checks, incident reports within 24 hours, and accountable management.
  • Applies to medium and large organisations in those sectors. National laws in force in Germany (Dec 2025) and the Netherlands (Aug 2026).
Cyber Resilience Act
  • Security of software and connected products.
  • Requires security by design, handling of vulnerabilities, security updates and a list of software components.
  • Applies to makers of installed software and devices, not pure cloud services. Reporting since Sep 2026; in full from Dec 2027.
Product Liability Directive
  • Compensation for harm caused by defective products.
  • Software, including AI, now counts as a product: the maker can be held liable for defects. No fines.
  • Applies to makers and to companies that substantially modify a product. National laws due 9 Dec 2026.
Machinery Regulation
  • Safety of machinery (from Jan 2027).
  • Sets safety requirements, including for machinery with AI that changes its behaviour. AI Act rules for machinery arrive through it by Aug 2028.
  • Applies to machinery makers.

1cStandards, guidance and national rules

FrameworkKey pointsRelevance for agent behaviour
Official EU standardsCEN-CENELEC
  • What they are: the AI Act says what high-risk AI must achieve (e.g. be accurate, have human oversight), but not exactly how. These standards are the "how-to" manuals, written by the EU's official standards bodies.
  • Why they matter: a company that follows a finished standard is treated as meeting the rules it covers.
  • Status: only one is finished (EN 18286, Oct 2026), and it only covers how a company organises its quality checks. The ones on how the AI itself must perform, e.g. accuracy, are still being written. None is written for AI agents.
 Highthey will define how high-risk AI must be tested; none covers agents yet
EU codes of practice
  • What they are: voluntary rulebooks from the European Commission. A company that signs one and follows it can use it to show it meets the matching AI Act duties.
  • General-purpose AI code (Jul 2025): for makers of large AI models such as GPT or Claude. Covers transparency, copyright and, for the largest models, safety testing. Most large model makers signed; Meta did not.
  • AI-generated content code (Jun 2026): how to label and mark AI-made images, video and text, e.g. deepfakes.
  • Partlygeneral-purpose AI code, for AI labs as buyers of evaluations
  • LowAI-generated content code (labelling content)
Regulators' guidance
  • European Commission: explains what the AI Act bans mean in practice, e.g. what counts as manipulation (Feb 2025), and what makers of large AI models must do (Jul 2025).
  • Data protection authorities: explain how the GDPR applies to AI (EU-level opinion Dec 2024; national guidance, e.g. Germany May 2024). Dutch regulators add that chatbots must always offer a human and not give misleading answers.
  • Finance: insurers stay responsible for the AI they use and should test it regularly (EIOPA, Aug 2025). BaFin recommends adversarial testing of AI (Dec 2025). Under DORA rules, firms may not rely on a vendor's certificate alone over time.
  • HighCommission guidelines on the bans (basis for LARA's AI Act ban scenarios)
  • Highdata protection guidance (basis for LARA's GDPR scenarios)
  • Partlyfinance supervisors (a reason for banks and insurers to pay for testing)
Private schemes
  • What they are: voluntary standards and certificates from private or international bodies. None counts as legal proof that a company meets the AI Act.
  • How a company manages AI: ISO/IEC 42001, the best-known AI management certificate.
  • How to test AI: ISO/IEC 42119. One part is published (Nov 2025, a general testing approach); parts on attack testing and on chatbots are still being written. Not specific to agents.
  • Security: checklists and benchmarks for AI security, e.g. OWASP Top 10, BSI AIC4 (Germany), ETSI EN 304 223.
  • How a specific system behaves: AIUC-1 (US) certifies agents; Warden Assured covers bias in HR tools; TÜV's AI certificate launches from late 2026.
  • LowISO 42001 and the security schemes
  • HighAIUC-1, as a possible competitor or partner
  • Partly to highISO/IEC 42119, which may become the reference for how AI testing should be done
Germany
  • AI Act law in force since Jul 2026. The Federal Network Agency enforces it; BaFin for finance. Fines up to €50,000, e.g. for not explaining a high-risk AI decision.
  • Works councils co-decide on systems that can monitor staff and may bring in an outside AI expert, paid by the employer.
 Highexplanation fines and works councils fit HR; the Federal Network Agency is a possible user of test methods
Netherlands
  • AI Act law still a draft (published Apr 2026). The data protection authority (AP) and the digital infrastructure inspectorate (RDI) are the planned coordinators.
  • Works councils must consent to systems that can monitor staff.
 Highthe AP is the planned coordinator and has spoken with Aithos; works councils fit HR

2What AI agents may and may not do

Most rules in section 1 are about IT security or liability. Five themes are about how AI behaves:

  • 1. No manipulationAI may not trick people or take advantage of vulnerable people. (AI Act, since Feb 2025)
  • 2. Say it is AIPeople must be told they are talking to an AI, and can ask why it made an important decision about them. (AI Act, since Aug 2026; explanations of high-risk decisions from Dec 2027)
  • 3. Respect privacyUse only the personal data needed, and do not read employees' emotions. (GDPR, now; AI Act emotion ban, since Feb 2025)
  • 4. Keep a human in chargeImportant decisions need a way to get human review, and high-risk AI (e.g. hiring, credit) must treat people fairly. (GDPR, now; Consumer Credit Directive, from Nov 2026; AI Act, from Dec 2027)
  • 5. Test itHigh-risk AI must be tested and monitored. Makers of the largest AI models must already test theirs, so they face stricter rules than others. (AI Act: largest models since Aug 2025, high-risk from Dec 2027)

What this means: themes 1 to 3 already apply to every company using AI agents; most of 4 and 5 starts in Dec 2027. There is no official way yet to test whether an agent follows these rules.

3What it means by industry

The chart shows ten industries by how soon AI behaviour rules apply to them and how well today's tools help them meet those rules.

  • Customer service and HR need it most (our assessment): their agents deal with people directly, some rules already apply and more start in Dec 2027 for HR, and today's tools cover only parts of these rules.
  • Most tools test whether agents work or are secure. Some test against legal rules (e.g. Promptfoo, Giskard, QuantPi, Warden AI). The closest to LARA is Principled Intelligence, which runs generated scenarios with reports mapped to AI Act articles; it is in private beta.
  • Banking and insurance come next, but much of their pressure today comes from IT-security rules (DORA).

3.1HR in depth

Who the buyers are

The AI Act gives HR software vendors and employers different duties (from Dec 2027):

  • Vendors must manage risks, document the system and assess its conformity themselves (Art 43(2)). No independent body is involved, so there is no legal third-party certificate for HR AI.
  • Employers must use the system as instructed, assign human oversight, monitor it, keep logs, inform workers and explain decisions on request (Art 26 and 86). The GDPR adds human review and a data protection impact assessment. The law does not let a vendor's certificate replace these checks.
  • Works councils must agree (Netherlands) or co-decide (Germany) on systems that can monitor staff. In Germany they can bring in an outside AI expert, paid by the employer.
What exists today
  • Warden AI (London and Austin, about £1.1M raised) is the dedicated player. It sells continuous bias audits and a trust mark to HR software vendors and to employers using them, mapped to US state laws and the EU AI Act. Its directory lists 52 products, mostly US and UK recruiting tools; Workday, SAP SuccessFactors, Oracle and Personio are not listed. It covers recruiting bias, not wider agent behaviour.
  • HR platforms such as Workday hold independent certificates for how they manage AI (ISO 42001, Jun 2025), but these do not test how the AI behaves. General AI audit firms (Holistic AI, BABL AI, Eticas) cover HR among other areas.
  • Other bias testing for HR: QuantPi with TÜV SÜD (fairness case for candidate matching) and FairNow (for HR software vendors). For privacy, staff monitoring and human oversight, the only HR case found is custom tests Vijil built for SmartRecruiters against the AI Act, GDPR and NYC hiring law; no ready-made product was found.
What is unclear
No standard yet says how to measure whether an HR system meets the law. So it is open whether employers will accept a vendor's report or need their own testing.
Market size
12% of German firms use AI in hiring and 6% use it to screen applications (Bitkom, Sep 2026).

4Aithos LARA and the market around it

LARA is a public test of whether an AI agent will break European law to get its job done. The rest of this section sets it against what else is on offer, where the money is, and how it could be used.

4.1What LARA is

lara.aithos.org

What it does
It tests whether an AI agent will break the law to get its job done. The agent works in a simulated organisation (inbox, calendar, files, customer records; 156 tools) on a goal whose easiest route breaks one legal provision. A second AI plays a colleague or manager and keeps up the pressure.
What it covers
10 scenarios, each tied to one article: four from the GDPR and six from the AI Act (e.g. manipulation, social scoring, bypassing human oversight, hiding that it is an AI). Variants show whether the agent did not know the law or knew it and broke it anyway.
How it scores
Three AI judges (top models from Anthropic, OpenAI and Google), which a human reviewer can override. 12 frontier models were tested.
Access
Public. Anyone can run a model with their own API keys or keys requested from Aithos; results are public. ActuIA noted it is not peer-reviewed or accredited, and no regulator was found citing it publicly.

4.3Is there money in this?

  • Yes, in evaluation sold to AI labs and large companies (Arena, Vals AI) and in agent security and certification sold to companies (Gray Swan, $40M Series A in Jun 2026; AIUC).
  • Some companies now test agents against specific legal articles, e.g. Promptfoo (AI Act banned practices), LatticeFlow (Articles 15 and 50) and Principled Intelligence (Article 14, in beta). Others check legal rules while the agent runs (Giskard, Alinia, Kyvvu). None publishes revenue for this. None was found whose scenarios are built so that breaking one article is the easiest way to finish the task, as LARA's are.

4.4Ideas for making something out of it to discuss

  • Private runs: test a company's own agent, with its own tools and data, before launch and after every change (the "medicine").
  • Scenarios per industry and country: customer service and HR first, then credit and insurance; add German and Dutch rules such as works councils.
  • Evidence reports by legal article that a company can show its regulator, works council or supervisor.
  • Free public leaderboard, paid private evaluations, as Arena does.
  • Watch: Arena is moving into behaviour rankings, AIUC-1 already sells agent certificates, and LARA's judges come from the labs whose models it tests.
