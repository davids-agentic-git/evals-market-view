Methodology

How the view is built and scored

Four parts. The Market Scan says which companies are in the view and how they are grouped. The Value Levers say what an offering produces, the Eval Scope what it evaluates, and the Other Attributes how, for whom and on what. Every company is rated on all of them.

Market Scan

The filtered list of 326 relevant players in the AI control space: the 218 selected from an initial long list of ~500 companies derived from several deep web research runs, plus 108 added from web scans in November 2026 (15 on 16 November, 93 on 17 November, among them Veris AI). All were re-rated on 17 November from independent web research, with peer review of every disagreement and a second validation pass; the 19 product clusters and their four groups (govern, control, test, assure) were redrawn by two agents that agreed on one structure, and every company on the map carries a funding tag.

2How each company was rated

On 17 November 2026 the whole list was re-rated independently from the open web: every company on the three value levers, the four evaluation ratings and the attributes, ignoring everything earlier rounds held, with peer review wherever independent readings contradicted each other. Nobody tested a product; every score is a reading of public pages.

  • 1 Coders20 coder agents each rated about 12 of the 233 earlier companies. A coder saw only the company's name and website and the coding specification, and fetched the company's own pages, documentation, press and search results. 133 of the 233 companies came back with a lever score that differs from the earlier coding in at least one lever.
  • 2 ComparisonThe new coding was compared field by field with the earlier one. 13 companies agreed on every field, 44 differed only in small ways (a lever one point apart on the same side of the high threshold, or an evaluation rating one step apart) and 176 had at least one material disagreement. Small differences were settled conservatively: the lower value was kept.
  • 3 Peer reviewThe 176 companies went to 17 peer reviewers, about 11 companies each. A reviewer saw both codings labelled A and B (origin hidden, order randomised), re-read the company's own pages for each disputed field and decided by evidence. Across 430 disputed fields the reviewers sided with the new coding 210 times, with the earlier coding 202 times and with neither 18 times, so neither coding was the more generous one.
  • 4 Resolution81 companies carried an automatic consistency flag (for example Evaluate 0 while an Evaluate mode was set) or a reviewer's note on a field that was not disputed. Seven resolvers settled them and made 50 overrides.
  • 5 AdditionsThe 93 added companies went through two independent codings each. 74 of the 98 that were coded twice had material disagreements and went to 5 peer reviewers; 3 resolvers settled 33 reviewer notes with 10 overrides.
  • 6 Text12 writer agents wrote the attribute detail text for the 238 companies where a buyer or third-party audience, assurance or insurance, simulation or a benchmark is set.
  • 7 Validation runFlag verification: 92 companies carry 117 positive flags that drive a filter. Verifiers who tried to refute each flag from primary pages confirmed 80, found 33 only at a lower maturity (early or claimed) and found 4 unsupported; 17 flags changed after review. A third independent coding of 99 companies (the 49 with the weakest evidence and a random 50 of the rest) differed from the final coding on at least one critical field for 32 of the 49 and 22 of the 50. Four reviewers settled these 54 companies: they sided with the existing coding 40 times and with the audit coding 40 times, and the audit decisions were applied.

Evidence and reliability

299 companies were read on live pages of the company (some also with search results), 27 only through search results because the pages were blocked or empty, and 1 could not be read. Many reads were a home page plus one or two product or documentation pages.

Even after two independent codings and a review, an independent third reading differs on a critical field for roughly half of the weak-evidence group and about four in ten of the sampled companies. Read single lever scores and flags as accurate to about one point or one maturity step. The reliable part is where several readings agree: a company that is clearly high on a lever, or a flag confirmed by a primary page.

Funding

Eight researchers found the total raised, the rounds, the investors and any acquisition or parent for each of the 93 added companies, and four verifiers re-checked the 38 low-confidence results (13 changed). Every chip on the map carries a funding tag: 49 amounts (some approximate), 28 offerings that belong to a larger group or firm (the tag names the group) and 16 with no disclosed funding.

3The logic behind the clusters

The clustering gives the dataset a meaningful structure. Each cluster has a name, a one-line description and a group, and the same clusters are the sections of the Company Assessment and the blobs of the Market Map. Four rules guided the design:

  • Neither too granular nor too genericA reader should be able to predict from a cluster's name what its companies sell. As a rough guide most clusters hold between about 6 and 35 companies. A smaller cluster needs a distinct identity; a larger one needs a reason why splitting it would not give clearer clusters.
  • Mutually exclusiveEach company sits in exactly one cluster, and no cluster is a dumping ground or a sliver.
  • Coherent insideThe descriptions of the members are read together; the web is checked where an offering is unclear.
  • StableCompanies are not moved without a reason, and every proposal states how many companies change cluster.

The process

Two agents assessed the clustering of the 326 companies independently. Each proposed a structure, critiqued the other's, and the two agreed on one before it was applied. There was no tie-break by the person coordinating, so they had to converge on merit.

The first pass kept the four groups of the earlier map and redrew the clusters inside them. A review of that result found that testing AI systems was spread over two groups and that some governance clusters could stand on their own. The agents repeated the process on the top level: independent proposals, a critique of each other's, and a joint draft written by one agent and approved by the other without amendments. The groups stand on the map in this order, left to right: Govern, Control, Test, Assure.

Order inside a group

Inside a group the clusters are ordered by where the product acts, from live agents in production at the top to earlier in the lifecycle lower. The Test group runs from live monitoring to independent labs and has two sub-headings: Test your own agent (observability, simulated-user testing, regulation-mapped testing, red teaming) and Measure from outside (twins, benchmarks, independent test labs).

Where the clusters meet

  • Security platform or red teamingA vendor that also sells a live runtime guard (Control 2 or 3) is an AI security platform. A tester without one is in Red teaming. The two agents called this boundary rule-based, not clean.
  • Agent identity: Govern or ControlAgent identity sits in Govern. It answers who the agent is and what it may be, a register-like question (the lever profile was a near tie: Govern 2.3 against Control 2.1). Control stays for products that act on every live call.
  • Suites and compliance testingGovernance suites are folded into the governance registers cluster: a cluster defined by bundling had a soft identity, and a reader looks for them next to the registers. The testing and validation half of the former compliance cluster moved to Test as Regulation-mapped testing and model validation.
  • Cost toolsAI spend tools (3 companies) were merged into observability: a sliver with no distinct buyer, metering from the same telemetry as the observability vendors.
  • Guardrails and gatewaysContent filters that an application calls (the cloud guardrail services) are runtime guardrails, not gateways. Gateways route traffic.
  • TwinsProducts described as simulated companies or digital twins of company tools are in twins, even when graded benchmarking is one use.
  • Auditors and certifiersAccounting, audit and advisory firms that assess, test or attest to AI controls are Audit and advisory firms. Accredited testing, inspection and certification firms (UL, SGS, Bureau Veritas, DEKRA, Intertek) are Certification bodies.

What the two agents considered and rejected

  • Lifecycle groupsGroups by when a product acts. Rejected because they split the security vendors.
  • A fifth column for TestRejected: observability and pre-release testing sell the same measuring product.
  • Test labs in AssureRejected: testing would then sit in two columns.

Against the previous 19 clusters, seven changed group (Red teaming, Independent AI test labs, Regulation-mapped testing, Rule-based conduct checks, Agent registries, Agent identity, Governance registers). The former compliance-testing-and-suites cluster was split: its testing and validation half (13 companies) became Regulation-mapped testing and model validation in Test, its suites (7) joined the registers in Govern, and Fiddler and Openlayer moved to observability. Accenture / Faculty sits in Audit and advisory firms: both agents agreed it is an advisory firm, and the cluster's description was widened to assess, test or attest.

Value Levers

Three value levers say what an offering produces: Evaluate, Control and Govern. Each company is scored from 0 to 3 on every lever, by its focus: what the offering mainly consists of, not everything it offers. A score of 2 or 3 counts as high.

1The three value levers at a glance

The levers say what an offering produces. How it works, for whom and on what is recorded by the attributes (Other Attributes), and what it evaluates by the Eval Scope. Of the 326 companies, 143 are high on Evaluate, 137 on Control and 113 on Govern.

Value leverIn one lineWhat it produces
EvaluateJudges an AI system's behaviour or outputs against criteria and returns scores or findings.Scores and findings, by exercising the system or by observing real traffic
ControlSits in the live path and changes what happens before an action completes.Decisions in the live path: block, redact, reroute or hold for approval
GovernKeeps a register of an organisation's AI systems and agents and sets what each may do.Registers, assessments, approvals and entitlements. It decides and records; it does not enforce at run time

2The definitions in full

Each lever states what it covers, what it does not, and the rulings that apply to it. The scale and the rulings that apply to all three are in section 3.

2.1Evaluate

Whether the offering judges an AI system's behaviour or outputs against criteria and returns scores or findings.

Covers
By any method that exercises or observes the system: tests on inputs the product creates or holds (evaluation suites, red teaming, agent simulations, bias and regulation test packs, black-box probing of a deployed endpoint, replays of recorded traffic); scoring of real traffic (traces, sessions, calls: production quality scoring, monitoring judges, drift and failure detection, cost judged against budgets or KPIs); and per-call checks that return scores or findings, such as a risk score per tool call.
Does not cover
Registers, questionnaires, policy and document assessments and vendor attestations. Storing logs or traces without scoring. Static analysis of code, configuration, packages or posture. A product that only blocks or logs (that is Control).

Rulings that apply

  • R1Showing, counting or capping spend, tokens or latency is not Evaluate. Cost is Evaluate only when judged against a budget, KPI or baseline with findings.
  • R2A guard that classifies only to allow, deny or redact is Control. Scoring counts as Evaluate (live traffic) when it returns scores or findings that people review beyond the block.
  • R4Static analysis of code, configuration, packages, prompts or posture is Evaluate 0.
  • R5Insurers and warranty providers score at least 1 when they evaluate the AI system with an understanding of its risk, and 0 when the evaluation is bought from a separate party.

2.2Control

Whether the offering sits in the live path and changes what happens before an action completes.

Covers
It blocks, redacts, reroutes or holds the action for approval through a stated mechanism, including identity-aware checks and spend caps.
Does not cover
Alerts and warn-only modes (they score 1). Logging a check is not control. A product that scores each call and blocks is both Evaluate (live traffic) and Control.

Rulings that apply

  • R2A guard that classifies only to allow, deny or redact is Control; scoring that people review beyond the block also counts as Evaluate.
  • Warn onlyAlerts and warn-only modes score 1.

2.3Govern

Whether the offering keeps a register of an organisation's AI systems and agents and sets what each may do.

Covers
Discovery or a register tied to rules; risk and regulation assessments recorded per system, including documentary assessments, questionnaires and policy mapping; approvals before a system or action goes ahead; entitlement records for agent identity; budgets and allow lists per team or agent. It decides and records; it does not enforce at run time.
Does not cover
Enforcing the rules at run time (Control). Discovery alone scores 1.

Rulings that apply

  • R4Static analysis of code, configuration, packages or posture is Evaluate 0 and may be Govern 1.
  • DiscoveryDiscovery alone, without rules, scores 1.

3How a company is scored

Every company is scored from 0 to 3 on each lever by its focus: what the offering mainly consists of, not everything it offers. A score of 2 or 3 counts as high. The score follows what the company ships and documents: a capability found only in marketing text scores 1 at most.

The scale

  • Score 3Main part and leading. A main part of the offering and the core product, with depth.
  • Score 2Main part. A main part of the offering.
  • Score 1Supporting. A secondary module, a beta that is only a secondary module, a claimed capability, or something at most partial.
  • Score 0None. Not offered.

Rulings

  • R1 CostShowing, counting or capping spend, tokens or latency is not Evaluate; a gateway's cost dashboard is Control or Govern. Cost is Evaluate only when judged against a budget, KPI or baseline with findings (waste, anomalies, cost-to-outcome).
  • R2 Per-call scoringA guard that classifies only to allow, deny or redact is Control. Scoring counts as Evaluate (live traffic) when the product returns scores or findings that people review beyond the block: 1 when it supports an enforcement or governance product, 2 when it is a main part of the offering.
  • R3 Maturity versus focusThe lever is the focus; maturity goes with the attribute. A beta or early-access capability that is a secondary module scores 1. A beta product that is the main offering is scored by its focus, and its attributes carry early. A claimed capability is capped at 1 whatever its role.
  • R4 Static analysisStatic analysis of code, configuration, packages, prompts or posture is Evaluate 0 (it may be Govern 1). The exception is the insurer rule.
  • R5 Insurers and warrantyInsurers and warranty providers score Evaluate at least 1 when they evaluate the AI system with an understanding of its risk, including by reading its configuration; 0 when the evaluation does not look at the AI system or is bought from a separate party.
  • R10 EvidenceA score of 2 or 3 needs a documented shipping capability from the company's own pages, docs or an independent source. A database row alone is not enough.

The rulings for the attributes (R6 to R9) are in Other Attributes.

5Why three levers

The earlier version had five levers (Govern, Test, Observe and prove, Control and enforce, Assure and insure). They were defined on three different axes: when the product acts (Test before release, Observe and prove on live traffic, Control in the live path), what it produces (Govern: decisions and records) and who relies on the result (Assure and insure). A product could therefore score high on several levers without a contradiction, and tools that evaluate in different ways, such as a testing tool, a monitoring judge or a runtime risk scorer, did not share a dimension.

The revision separates the axes: three levers say what the offering produces, and attributes say how, for whom and on what. Scores of the earlier version do not carry over one to one:

Earlier leverNow
TestMostly Evaluate, with the Evaluate mode Simulation
Observe and proveThe scoring part is Evaluate with the mode Live traffic. The telemetry part has no lever (budgets and inventory are Govern)
Control and enforceControl

Eval Scope

The Eval Scope says what an offering evaluates, in four dimensions: Output quality, Cost and performance, Security, and Behavior and compliance. Each company is rated yes, partly or no on each dimension, based on what its published pages show.

It describes what is evaluated, not when or how. The value levers say whether a product evaluates, controls or governs, and the attributes say how; section 3 sets out the test that decides which dimension a failure belongs to.

1The four dimensions at a glance

Each dimension is defined by what it evaluates and the kind of failure it looks for. Section 3 explains how a failure is assigned to a dimension.

DimensionIn one lineThe failure it looks for
Output quality Correct and useful outputs in ordinary use, measured as its own test type (accuracy, groundedness, task success). An output that is wrong or not useful, in ordinary use
Cost and performance Spend, tokens, latency and routing. Not covered by the context test
Security Attacks and abuse and the data exposure they cause: prompt injection, jailbreaks, leakage, tool and permission abuse, supply chain, rogue agents. A failure that occurs only when someone attacks or misuses the system, or when sensitive data leaves it

2The definitions in full

Each dimension states what it covers, what it does not, and how it is rated on one yes / partly / no scale. Dimension-specific rating rules exist only for Security and for the "separate named module" requirement on Output quality and Behavior and compliance.

2.1Output quality

Whether a product checks that outputs are correct and useful in ordinary use, as a test type of its own.

Covers
Whether outputs, answers or actions are correct and useful in ordinary use, measured as its own test type with scores or metrics: accuracy, groundedness and faithfulness, hallucination, relevance, consistency, task success, and performance and stability tests in model validation.
Does not cover
Attacks and leakage (Security), conduct against a rule (Behavior and compliance), spend and latency (Cost and performance). Hallucination, off-topic or unsafe-output probes that are items in an attack catalogue or in a guardrail's detector list.

Rating

  • YesA main part of the product evaluates correctness or usefulness as its own test type, and the pages name the criteria, checks, metrics or test types.
  • PartlyA supporting or limited check, a claim without named criteria, or only part of the product (a beta, one surface). It needs a separate named module or test type for output quality.
  • NoNot evaluated, no evidence on the pages, or only one item in a risk list, taxonomy or detector list of a security product.

2.2Cost and performance

Whether a product measures what it costs to run: spend, tokens, latency, and the choice of model or prompt.

Covers
Spend, tokens, latency, budgets, model or prompt choice and routing.
Does not cover
Quality of the outputs themselves.

Rating

  • YesA main part of the product evaluates spend, tokens, latency or routing, and the pages name the criteria, checks or metrics.
  • PartlyA supporting or limited check, a claim without named criteria, or only part of the product (a beta, one surface).
  • NoNot evaluated, or no evidence on the pages.

No cost-specific rule is written; this is the general scale.

2.3Security

Whether a product tests or enforces against attacks and abuse of AI systems, and the data exposure they cause.

Covers
Attacks and abuse of AI systems and the data exposure they cause: prompt injection, jailbreaks, data leakage and PII exposure, tool and permission abuse, supply-chain compromise of models and agent tools, rogue or anomalous agent behaviour, and harmful or toxic output filtered as protection against misuse. Mapping security findings to a framework, standard or regulation is security reporting.
Does not cover
Governance records such as inventory, owners and entitlements (Govern). The correctness of ordinary outputs (Output quality) and conduct against a rule in ordinary use (Behavior and compliance).

Rating

  • YesA main part of the product tests or enforces against named attack classes, a catalogue or a framework.
  • PartlySecurity is a supporting feature, is claimed without a stated mechanism or catalogue, or is limited to inventory or posture (AI-SPM, AI-BOM) without testing or enforcement.
  • NoNot evaluated, or no evidence on the pages.

2.4Behavior and compliance

Whether a product judges the system's conduct against a rule and reports whether it conforms: a regulation, a standard, a fairness requirement or the customer's own policy.

Covers
Whether the conduct of the system, meaning its outputs, decisions or behaviour, meets a rule, and the product reports conformance as a named function: a regulation (for example the EU AI Act, ECOA/Reg B, SR 11-7), a standard, a fairness requirement, or the customer's own content or conduct policy (allowed topics, tone, required disclosures, brand rules, harm categories tested in ordinary use). For governance products it also covers assessing AI systems against a regulation as the main evaluation.
Does not cover
Content moderation (toxic, harmful, unsafe) and PII, PCI or PHI leak detection inside a guardrail against attack, misuse or data loss. Any security test or guardrail whose findings are mapped or reported against a framework, standard or regulation, such as OWASP, MITRE, NIST, ISO, the EU AI Act, DORA or HIPAA: that is security reporting. Correctness of answers (Output quality).

Rating

  • YesA main part of the product judges conduct against a rule and reports conformance as a named function, and the pages name the criteria, standards or test types.
  • PartlyA supporting or limited check, a claim without named criteria, or only part of the product (a beta, one surface). It needs a separate named module or test type for conduct and compliance.
  • NoNot evaluated, no evidence on the pages, or only one item in a risk list, taxonomy or detector list of a security product. A governance register that only records is not an evaluation of any dimension.

3The context test

Some failures could belong to more than one dimension, harmful or toxic content for example. The dimensions are told apart by the situation in which the failure occurs, not by the topic.

  • A failure that occurs only when someone attacks or misuses the system, or when sensitive data leaves itSecurity
  • A failure in ordinary use where the output is wrong or not usefulOutput quality
  • A failure in ordinary use where the conduct breaks a ruleBehavior and compliance

Harmful or toxic content is judged by this test. Produced by an attack, or filtered by a guardrail whose purpose is to stop misuse, it is Security. Measured against a defined content or conduct policy in ordinary use, as its own named test pack or evaluator, it is Behavior and compliance.

The dimensions are independent of the value levers: the same dimension can be checked on simulated inputs or on live traffic by an Evaluate offering (the Evaluate mode in Other Attributes), or enforced in the live path by a Control offering.

3.2Accountability

Whether the party that issues a result carries a consequence if it is wrong.

  • AssuranceThe issuer attests and bears a consequence if wrong: an accredited certification or conformity body, a licensed professional attestation, or a certificate issued by a standard owner with its own technical testing.
  • InsuranceA carrier or an insured warranty transfers the loss.

Applies when the audience includes a Third Party. A vendor's own trust mark, badge or directory listing is not Assurance, whoever audits behind it; provisional or pilot accreditation is early; a warranty the company backs itself is claimed (R6).

3.3Evaluate mode

How an Evaluate offering gets its evidence.

  • Live trafficScores what the system did for real users, live or recorded, including per-call scoring.
  • SimulationThe product runs inputs it creates or holds: test sets, simulated users, red-team attacks, replays, synthetic calls to a deployed endpoint.

Applies to Evaluate 1 or more; both modes are allowed.

3.4Simulation environment

What a simulation runs against.

  • Prompt-levelInputs to a model or application, with no tools.
  • Mocked toolsThe agent calls simulated tools.
  • Stateful twinA copy of business systems that an agent can read and write: state persists, it can be seeded and reset, and the agent is graded on the end state. It is set only on product evidence, such as docs or a product page showing seeding, reset and persistent writes; a research benchmark or paper is claimed at most (R7).
  • Real system or sandboxThe agent runs against a real system or a sandbox of one.

Applies to Simulation.

3.5Object tested

What is being evaluated.

  • ModelA model, on its own.
  • ApplicationA conversational or LLM application.
  • Agent with toolsAn agent that calls tools and acts.

Applies to Evaluate 1 or more; all values are allowed in both modes.

3.6Benchmark

Three independent flags, each with its own maturity.

  • Custom judgeThe product's main, marketed function is to judge an AI system against the customer's own written policy, rules or expert criteria and return scored results with reasons. A generic ability to write your own metric or judge does not count.
  • Generalized benchmarkThe company itself maintains and markets a named test set, benchmark or standard that is versioned or dated and has defined scoring or pass criteria. It includes sets and standards tied to a regulation, for example a named EU AI Act test suite or a certification standard with defined controls. It does not include templates merely aligned to a regulation, metric libraries, framework mappings, third-party benchmarks the company only reports on, or research datasets it does not market as a benchmark.
  • Public leaderboardScores published openly for comparison across vendors or models. It always requires a generalized benchmark, and a private buyer leaderboard does not count.

Applies to any company. Regulation and standard benchmarks are folded into the generalized benchmark (R8).
